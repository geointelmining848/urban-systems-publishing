# Stage 6 - Submission Package

Assemble the materials the **specific journal and article type** require. A useful package
makes contribution, audience fit, evidence, and disclosures easy to assess. It does not
compensate for an unsupported claim. Drafting a package is not itself authorization to
submit a manuscript, contact editors/reviewers, upload restricted data, or make declarations
on an author's behalf. Respect any explicit authorization already supplied.

Check current official requirements using [journal-profiles.md](journal-profiles.md).
Do not use corpus medians, a publisher-wide assumption, or remembered word limits as the
compliance standard. If a requirement is unavailable, deliver the prepared materials and
identify that exact unresolved item.

## Cover letter

Make the argument for this readership in plain, specific prose. Address a named editor
only when the current role and intended recipient are verified; "Dear Editor" is suitable
otherwise. Lead with the contribution the evidence earns:

- A conceptual advance: which debate changes, and how the evidence or argument supports it.
- A substantive or population finding: who/what is affected, magnitude and uncertainty
  where relevant, and why the readership needs it.
- A method, prediction, or measurement advance: what limitation it addresses, what
  validation supports it, and how others can use it.

A quantitative finding may carry a conceptual argument; a descriptive study can have
policy relevance without identifying intervention efficacy. Avoid fixed assumptions that
an urban-geography editor cares only about theory or that an economics editor accepts only
causal empirical work.

The letter normally answers these questions, in a natural order suited to the venue:

1. What is being submitted, and what does it contribute?
2. Why does the contribution fit this journal's readers and article type?
3. What evidence makes the main claim credible, and what limit matters to its interpretation?
4. What declarations, related-work disclosures, or other information does the venue require?

Use one or two verified relevant publications or a precise scope connection when helpful.
Do not invent a recent debate, flatter the editor, recite a generic novelty/impact rubric,
or promise a treatment benefit the study did not estimate.

```text
Dear Editor,

Please consider "[Title]" as a [article type] in [Journal].

[Specific contribution and its evidential basis, in one or two sentences.]
[Why that contribution matters to this journal's readers; a relevant limit if needed.]

[Required declarations, included only from author-confirmed information.]
[Related manuscripts/preprints, permissions, data-access conditions, or other relevant
information required by the current guide.]

Sincerely,
[Confirmed corresponding author]
```

## Declarations: verified facts, not boilerplate assumptions

Ask for missing author facts only when needed to finalize a declaration. Until confirmed,
label them as outstanding rather than assert them. In particular:

- Author approval, authorship order, affiliations, corresponding-author identity, and
  contributions must reflect the authors' actual agreement. Do not infer consent from
  possession of the draft.
- Originality, exclusive consideration, related manuscripts, prior conference versions,
  and preprints require accurate disclosure under the target's rules. A preprint is not
  automatically prohibited prior publication; verify the policy.
- Funding, competing interests, industry relationships, and assistance must be stated
  truthfully. Check venue-specific tobacco, cannabis, alcohol, pharmaceutical, or other
  relevant funding policies without transferring one journal's exclusions to an entire field.
- Ethics approval/exemption, consent, data-use permissions, and registration identifiers
  require supplied or verified documentation. Do not invent an approval or assume that
  aggregation makes sensitive geographies unrestricted.
- Follow applicable publisher policies on AI assistance and human accountability; verify
  current wording and the actual use. Do not claim that an assistant is an author or that
  authors verified facts they have not checked.

## Lay, policy, and significance summaries

Verify whether the submission requires a plain-language summary, significance statement,
research-in-context panel, highlights, or another format. Requirements vary by journal
and article type. Do not infer that every Nature journal uses Nature's summary paragraph,
every health journal has the same structured abstract, or every geographic-health journal
follows AGU's format. Use [significance-and-framing.md](significance-and-framing.md) and the
applicable [reporting-standards.md](reporting-standards.md).

For a non-specialist summary, communicate question, approach, main finding, and meaning
without jargon. Name the place, population, and limits that matter. Translate units
carefully: an odds ratio of 0.68 describes lower odds, not automatically a one-third lower
risk. Do not turn an area-level association into a statement about an individual person,
or a forecast scenario into a prediction guaranteed to occur.

A policy summary can explain what the findings inform, which options remain uncertain,
and what additional evidence would support intervention decisions. It must not strengthen
the claim beyond the manuscript. A summary in another language must preserve the same
uncertainty and inference type.

## Reviewer suggestions and exclusions

Prepare suggestions only if requested by the journal or user. Use verified public
professional information and relevant expertise, not guessed contact details. Check the
journal's current conflict criteria; a universal three- or four-year coauthorship rule is
not reliable across venues.

Consider recent collaborators/coauthors, institutional relationships, supervisory ties,
financial interests, and other conflicts under the applicable policy. Suggest the mix of
expertise the study needs: spatial methods, substantive domain, theory, policy, or other
relevant expertise. Avoid selecting only allies or people expected to be favorable.

Give specific, factual reasons for exclusions when legitimate. Do not manufacture a
conflict to avoid criticism. Do not contact proposed reviewers to solicit a favorable
report or supply an unrequested manuscript. Editors retain the reviewer-selection decision.

## Data and code availability

Use [data-and-reproducibility.md](data-and-reproducibility.md) to prepare an accurate
statement. Verify repository links/identifiers, versions, access conditions, and permissions.
Check the target's rules for data/software citation and preservation; a live code hosting
page may not satisfy a requirement for an archived release.

For restricted data, state what is restricted, why, the legitimate access route if one
exists, and what can be shared. Code, synthetic examples, metadata, and permissible
aggregates can improve reproducibility, but do not claim a synthetic-data demonstration
reproduces private empirical results. Some data have no general controlled-access pathway;
say so rather than invent one. Aggregation, coarsening, or removal of names alone does not
guarantee safe release of geospatial or health data.

## Presubmission enquiry

Check the exact venue/article type and its current policy. Enquiries may be permitted,
discouraged, unavailable, or intended only for proposed non-research formats. Do not assume
a common policy for Nature, Lancet, or another publisher family, or promise a response time.

When appropriate, draft a concise contribution summary, fit rationale, evidential basis,
and requested materials. Include enough design information to assess the claim. An
encouraging response is not a promise of review or acceptance. Sending the enquiry requires
user authorization for that external communication; preparation can proceed independently.

and claims before declaring the package ready. Deliver the materials requested, the
compliance findings, and the smallest remaining factual or policy gaps. Do not claim the
manuscript has been submitted unless the authorized action actually succeeded.
